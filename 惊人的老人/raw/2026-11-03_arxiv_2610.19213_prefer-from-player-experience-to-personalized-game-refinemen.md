# PREFER: From Player Experience to Personalized Game Refinement
###### Abstract
Understanding how large language models (LLMs) evaluate gameplay experience is important for using their feedback to guide game design. However, overall ratings alone provide limited insight into which aspects of experience are associated with these judgments and how they can inform concrete game changes. We introduce Prefer, a framework that uses a shared set of experience dimensions adapted from established psychometric questionnaires to benchmark how models evaluate gameplay and to guide persona-specific game refinement. Through these dimensions, we characterize the perspectives models offer as virtual playtesters by examining how their overall assessments relate to specific aspects of experience and to the gameplay they produce. To examine how the same dimensions can guide refinement for players with different preferences, we use personas to simulate distinct player profiles. For each persona, Prefer uses experience-preference relationships to select refinement objectives, revises game rules and their implementations, and playtests candidate games to assess experience changes and predicted preference gains. Experiments with five personas show that Prefer achieves 9.2% higher mean post-play preference ratings than the strongest evaluated baseline.
## 1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in game development, supporting the generation of playable prototypes and the evaluation of player experience (Sun et al., 2024; Lankes and Stöckl, 2023). Gameplay experience involves multiple aspects, such as challenge, mastery, and immersion (Sweetser and Wyeth, 2005; Vanden Abeele et al., 2020). Understanding how these aspects relate to models’ overall judgments is therefore important for characterizing the perspectives they offer as virtual playtesters and for using their feedback to guide game refinement.
Established player-experience instruments, such as PXI and CORGIS, provide structured dimensions for describing functional, psychosocial, and challenge-related aspects of gameplay (Vanden Abeele et al., 2020; Denisova et al., 2020). Building on such instruments, Dutta et al. (2026) map player reviews to experience constructs and examine their associations with review scores. In parallel, MeepleLM generates ratings and critiques from game rulebooks under different player personas, illustrating how LLMs can provide feedback from multiple player perspectives (Li et al., 2026).
Building on these advances, we ask how different LLMs evaluate the experience reflected in actual gameplay. An overall rating alone does not reveal whether models agree on specific aspects of an experience, or how those assessments relate to their overall judgments. Characterizing their perspectives therefore calls for comparing both levels of evaluation and examining how these judgments relate to the gameplay the models produce. Beyond this analysis, a further question is whether the same experience dimensions can guide game refinement for players with different preferences, by specifying which aspects to change and in which direction.
To address these questions, we introduce Prefer, a framework that uses shared experience dimensions to benchmark how models evaluate gameplay and to guide persona-specific game refinement. We adapt experience dimensions from established psychometric questionnaires to assess gameplay trajectories, collecting overall ratings separately from dimension-level assessments. Different models evaluate the same trajectories, allowing us to examine how their overall judgments relate to specific aspects of experience and how their assessments agree or differ. We further compare their evaluations of trajectories produced through their own play and that of other models, and examine agreement in game-level rankings.
However, players differ in what they value in a game (Tondello et al., 2019). To better simulate diverse real-world players, we follow MeepleLM (Li et al., 2026) in using personas to represent distinct player profiles. For each persona, Prefer relates experience dimensions to overall preference to select which aspects to change and in which direction. These objectives guide rule revisions, which are translated into function-level contracts that specify required behavior and guide implementation checks and repair. Candidate games are then playtested under the target persona, and acceptance requires both the intended experience changes and a positive predicted preference gain. Feedback from unsuccessful candidates guides further rule revisions.
We evaluate Prefer on familiar card games and their rule variants. The model benchmark identifies shared patterns in game rankings alongside differences in how experience dimensions relate to overall ratings. Across five personas, Prefer achieves 9.2% higher mean post-play preference ratings than the strongest baseline evaluated. A separate implementation study also shows a higher success rate in producing runnable games and higher rule-consistency scores than baseline.
Our contributions are threefold:
We introduce Prefer, a framework that adapts experience dimensions from psychometric questionnaires to gameplay trajectories, providing a shared basis for model evaluation and persona-specific game refinement.
We establish a model benchmark that characterizes the perspectives LLMs offer as virtual playtesters by comparing their experience judgments, overall ratings, and game rankings, and relating these evaluations to the gameplay they produce.
We develop a persona-specific game refinement method that uses experience-preference relationships to select modification targets, guides rule revisions with playtesting feedback, and supports implementation through function-level contracts and execution checks.
## 2 Related Work
#### Game-Based Agent Evaluation.
Games let researchers observe how agents follow rules, choose actions, and respond to other players over several turns. clembench tests instruction following and task success through dialogue games (Kranti et al., 2023). GameBench studies strategic reasoning across nine games (Costarelli et al., 2024). TextArena offers competitive text games that involve skills such as negotiation and deception (Guertler et al., 2025). Our benchmark uses shared experience measures to compare models without persona prompts against reference players given fixed persona prompts. We compare their evaluation profiles on shared gameplay evidence and relate these judgments to the play the models produce.
#### Personas and Player Experience.
Persona prompts ask an agent to follow a given role or set of preferences. TRAIT measures model personality through multiple-choice questions about everyday situations (Lee et al., 2025). PERIL studies how persona prompts affect game strategies. It maps a model’s answers to personality questions into strategy parameters (Licato and Steinle, 2025). MeepleLM uses player personas to rate games and explain their judgments from rule descriptions (Li et al., 2026). PXI and CORGIS offer scales for player experience and perceived challenge (Vanden Abeele et al., 2020; Denisova et al., 2020). We use these scales to describe gameplay. Personas guide our game revisions and provide reference players for model comparison.
#### Game Refinement and Verification.
Game refinement requires both useful feedback and code that follows the revised rules. Self-Refine uses model feedback to improve generated outputs (Madaan et al., 2023) and systems (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026). Cardiverse creates card game variants and playable implementations (Li et al., 2025). Fly, Fail, Fix uses play records from a reinforcement learning agent to guide changes to game settings (Zook et al., 2025). For code repair, LDB checks execution step by step to locate errors (Zhong et al., 2024). PolyVer uses function contracts to verify programs written in different languages (Chen et al., 2025). In our method, a target player’s experience guides rule changes. Fixed function contracts guide code repair, and playtesting checks the resulting experience.
## 3 Method
Prefer uses shared experience dimensions to study how LLMs evaluate gameplay and to guide game refinement for a target persona. We first describe the experience assessment. We then explain how persona preferences guide rule changes, how function contracts guide their implementation, and how playtesting provides feedback for further revision.
### 3.1 Experience Assessment
We describe gameplay through selected dimensions from PXI (Vanden Abeele et al., 2020) and CORGIS (Denisova et al., 2020). These dimensions cover how a game supports and engages the player, as well as the demands of play. For example, challenge fit measures whether difficulty suits the player. Cognitive challenge measures the reasoning and planning required during play. We keep the dimension definitions and scoring scales fixed across models and personas. ## 4 Experiments
Our experiments connect three questions: what experience dimensions reveal about model evaluation, how persona-specific preference relationships define refinement targets, and whether the resulting game revisions receive higher post-play preference ratings. We examine each connection separately before following the complete process in a concrete case.
### 4.1 Experimental Setup
#### Model evaluation.
We benchmark GLM (Zeng et al., 2026), DeepSeek (Xu et al., 2026), Kimi (Team et al., 2026), MiniMax (Chen et al., 2026), and Qwen (Qwen Team, 2026) on 91 card games with fixed rules and implementations. The models play against random opponents without persona prompts. Five neutral judges and five persona references assess the recorded trajectories with actor identities hidden. Overall ratings and 12 experience ratings are collected in separate calls. Appendix A details model configurations and analysis sets.
#### Persona prediction and refinement.
We adopt five profiles from MeepleLM (Li et al., 2026): Efficiency Essentialist, Narrative Architect, Social Lubricator, System Purist, and Thrill Seeker. They provide reference evaluations for model comparison and serve as target players in game refinement. We validate preference predictions on held-out games and assess game revisions through persona playtesting and code implementation checks. Appendix B describes the evaluation protocols.
### 4.2 Model Evaluation through Experience Dimensions
We use experience dimensions to characterize how models evaluate recorded gameplay. Figure 3 presents two complementary views: the associations between individual dimensions and overall ratings, and the similarity of these association profiles to persona references.
We first examine whether models broadly agree on which games they rate highly. With complete rankings, four judges define high-, middle-, and low-rated groups for the fifth judge to compare. Each group spans ten rank positions. Each judge is held out in turn. All five favor the higher-rated group. Averaging the high–middle and middle–low comparisons gives mean gaps of 1.10-1.87 points and pairwise agreement of 78.4-96.0%, shown in Table 1. Figure 5 illustrates this pattern with the highest, middle, and lowest five games by pooled score. Each column orders the displayed games by one judge’s mean rating over actor sources, and lines connect the same game across judges. High-scoring games generally remain above low-scoring ones. At the level of overall ratings, the models show broad agreement.
We then compare how their overall ratings relate to experience dimensions and use persona references to interpret the resulting association profiles. Figure 3(a) shows positive mastery associations across models, alongside differences in immersion and challenge fit. The displayed mastery correlations range from 0.41 to 0.57, placing mastery among the two largest correlations for every model. The other prominent associations differ: GLM has an immersion correlation of 0.56, DeepSeek a challenge-fit correlation of 0.42, and Qwen a meaning correlation of 0.54. These patterns identify a common association with mastery while showing that the remaining dimensions relate differently to each model’s overall ratings.
The persona references provide a way to interpret these differences in evaluation emphasis. Figure 3(b) expresses these evaluation patterns relative to the five persona references. System has the largest estimated similarity share for GLM, DeepSeek, Kimi, and Qwen, while Thrill has the largest share for MiniMax. Similarity describes a combination of evaluation emphases for each model. For example, MiniMax’s shares are 25.9% for Thrill, 22.0% for Narrative, and 22.1% for System. These shares describe relative resemblance between evaluation profiles, without implying positive raw correlations. Social has the smallest normalized share for every neutral model. DeepSeek yields the largest share for Social, 13.2%, whereas the corresponding values for the other models range from 7.7% to 10.1%. This suggests that the pool of neutral judges may provide uneven coverage of different reference perspectives, highlighting the importance of persona-based perspectives.
### 4.3 Validating Experience-Based Preference Models
The persona profiles in Figure 4(a) characterize differences in evaluation emphasis on common benchmark inputs. For example, emotional challenge is positively associated with Thrill’s ratings and negatively associated with System’s. These patterns motivate preference predictors that combine experience dimensions for each persona. We next examine whether the same experience dimensions predict preference when games are played under the target persona. This analysis uses a separate dataset of 455 persona-played observations from the same 91 games and five personas, with experience scores and preference ratings collected separately.
#### Preference prediction.
We compare separate regularized linear predictors for each persona with a persona-mean baseline and a shared predictor that uses common dimension coefficients with persona-dependent score levels. Figure 4(b) reports persona-wise prediction errors. Averaged equally over the five personas, the separate predictors achieve a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.740, compared with 1.090 for the persona-mean baseline and 0.785 for the shared predictor. ### 4.4 Post-play Preference and Implementation Quality
We compare Prefer with Cardiverse’s game-generation workflow (Li et al., 2025), Reflexion’s self-feedback refinement (Shinn et al., 2023), ReCode’s bounded recursive planning (Yu et al., 2025), and PromptBreeder’s mutation (Fernando et al., 2024). We report mean post-play preference and implementation metrics in Cardiverse (Li et al., 2025): Success Rate, Execution Rate, code-rulebook embedding similarity (ECon), and play-rule consistency (PCon). Appendices B and D detail the adaptations, datasets, and protocols.
As shown in Table 2, Prefer achieves a mean preference of 3.333, a paired increase of 0.407 over the original games and 9.2% above the best baseline’s mean of 3.053. It also achieves the highest implementation success rate (86.67%) and PCon (7.377), with execution rate tied at 99.96%. The different method rankings under ECon and PCon highlight the value of checking actual gameplay alongside code-rulebook similarity.
Table 4 reports paired preference changes from the original games for both directed refinement and its feedback extension, averaged over 30 games per persona. Directed refinement achieves a mean gain of 0.380 points. Persona-level mean gains range from 0.067 for Narrative to 1.167 for Thrill. These differences may reflect how readily persona preferences can be addressed through local rule edits. Such edits can reshape the immediate risks and consequences relevant to Thrill, but may offer fewer opportunities to develop thematic progression for Narrative or improve strategic control and balance for System. The feedback extension yields a small additional improvement, increasing the mean paired preference gain from 0.380 to 0.407 points, an additional 0.027 points. Overall, Prefer improves mean post-play preference in the evaluated card games, with gains varying across personas.
### 4.5 Case Study: From an Experience Target to a Rule Change
Figure 6 follows a Crazy Eights revision for Thrill Seeker with a fixed challenge-fit target. Revealing a player’s hand after extended drawing leaves challenge fit unchanged. Feedback then guides a stop-or-continue drawing mechanic with a three-draw limit. In the resulting playtest, challenge fit rises from 2 to 3 and post-selection preference from 2 to 6. The case illustrates how feedback guides a concrete mechanic change toward the same experience target. Appendix C.1 gives the full rule changes and evaluation record.
## 5 Conclusion
Prefer uses shared experience dimensions to connect model evaluation with persona-guided game refinement. The dimensions reveal differences hidden by overall ratings, predict persona preference on held-out games, and define concrete modification targets. Delivered revisions receive higher mean post-play preference than the original games and evaluated baselines. The implementation evaluation also reports higher success and play-rule consistency. Together, these results demonstrate how experience measurements can inform concrete, executable game revisions.
